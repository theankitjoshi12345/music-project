Maddux Vitrano (MV): Um, I'd say a fairly even split, like rehearsing with the band and practicing on my own.

Interviewer (Int.): So how do you set up your music?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): If it's like a concert ensemble, usually they give it to us, print it out, and then they say, hey, we're gonna play these pieces.
Interviewer (Int.): So you use paper, not any digital device for it?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): If it's a concert ensemble, it's paper, but if it's a marching ensemble, then it's all digital.
Interviewer (Int.): All right. Now it comes to an interesting point. So when the director asks everyone to start at a particular measure or section or rehearsal mark, how do you follow it? Because on paper, it would be harder for you to get exactly to that point, right?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Yeah. I don't know, I kind of just eyeball them, search through for a little bit until I find it.
Interviewer (Int.): All right. So can you remember a recent time when finding the right place took longer than expected?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Yes, because sometimes the numbers are not written on there. So you find the nearest rehearsal number and then you count however many measures to the measure you actually have to start.
Interviewer (Int.): So tell me about the last time you lost your place or missed an entrance or felt like you were behind during the rehearsal because of that problem.
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Oh, me personally, I don't know, maybe like last year.
Interviewer (Int.): Last year? It doesn't happen that often?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): No.
Interviewer (Int.): You don't feel like you are behind?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): No, not me specifically. Some people, yeah.
Interviewer (Int.): Some people. So, do you know what caused it for them maybe?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): It's usually just like a lack of attention.
Interviewer (Int.): Lack of attention? Yeah. All right, so how many people have a problem like that a lot? Or maybe in terms of percentage?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): I mean, it happens to everybody every now and then, but consistently, I don't know, maybe like 20%.
Interviewer (Int.): So when you receive a piece that looks difficult, what do you normally do before you feel ready to play it?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): I go and I look at it. I try to tap out the hardest-looking parts, the stuff that I don't think I can do. Like, if I'm going through and they're like, here's the music, we're playing this in like 30 seconds, I go, I look at the hard stuff, figure it out, and then sight-read the rest, usually.
Interviewer (Int.): So do you add markings?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Depending on what it's for. Like, certain melodic pieces I will, if I have enough time.
Interviewer (Int.): So, do you practice certain measures, like some difficult ones?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Oh, yeah.
Interviewer (Int.): And you look for fingerings or notes during playing if you messed up something? You put up some notes?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Oh yeah, yeah, yeah.
Interviewer (Int.): All right. Do you ask somebody for help?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Usually not, unless I just can't figure it out for some reason.
Interviewer (Int.): Think about the last piece where you added a lot of markings to the piece. What did you add and why?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Well, for me, since I do melodic percussion, the last thing I added was just stickings for a melodic piece that didn't have the sticking written. Like, sometimes if I'm playing four-mallet marimba, I'll write down which sticks are hitting which notes.
Interviewer (Int.): All right. Approximately how much time did you spend preparing or marking that piece before a rehearsal?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): That's definitely when players do that way more often than us in percussion, but I don't know, for me, like maybe five minutes.
Interviewer (Int.): Five minutes. All right. This is still gonna save at least some time. So what tools do you normally use when practicing or rehearsing your music, like printed music, pencil or highlighter, binder, phone, tablet, laptop, GoodNotes? There is an app called MuseScore, maybe, and then SmartMusic or any other apps?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): For me, I usually just use, like, a metronome, maybe a pencil, eyeball it.
Interviewer (Int.): All right, let's talk about digital sheet music. So tell me about the last time you used a phone, tablet, or computer to read the music.
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Let's see. Yesterday, and in about 15 minutes, I will.
Interviewer (Int.): All right. So you use your phone, right? Not the laptop or anything?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): I definitely prefer the laptop, but I can't use it for marching because I can't put it on my drum.
Interviewer (Int.): So what app or file format do you use? PDF, most likely?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Yeah, we usually get the PDF.
Interviewer (Int.): All right. How did you get the music onto the device?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): A shared Google Drive.
Interviewer (Int.): So, Google Drive. What did you have to do to get the music ready to use? Do you have to import the file, crop it sometimes, zoom it, rearrange the pieces, edit some notation, change the orientation, or adjust the brightness?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): With the shared Google Drive, everything is just ready. Importing the file, maybe, just... If it wasn't a shared Google Drive, then definitely. But since they just use Google Drive for everything, it's already set up for us.
Interviewer (Int.): Okay. What parts of the process were inconvenient or took longer than you expected when using it on the phone?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): With Google Drive, jumping between different things takes a while. I have to go through and then I just save everything. I download everything to where it's all in the same place in the offline section so I can easily bounce between.
Interviewer (Int.): And if it is downloaded, it's not gonna take that long either, right?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Yeah. If you don't download it and you try to open it at the football games, you're not getting it. It's not happening.
Interviewer (Int.): Can you remember the last time you had to zoom, scroll, or change spaces while you were actually playing?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Every time. So Wednesday.
Interviewer (Int.): So did you keep playing?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Yeah, yeah.
Interviewer (Int.): How do you keep playing if you have to change it? You might have to stop a little bit.
Maddux Vitrano (MV): No, I wait until a part where I can do it with one hand and then I scroll with my other hand and then go back to playing.
Interviewer (Int.): All right. So you recover fast from it. When the director gives you a correction during the rehearsal, what do you normally do?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): I usually just keep it in my head and just fix it next time.
Interviewer (Int.): But you might forget it. If you're using Google Drive, they don't have a quick edit option, do they?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Yeah, I haven't. There's no editing on Google Drive.
Interviewer (Int.): So if there is somebody who makes a lot of mistakes and they want to correct it, they will need some sort of tool that Google Drive is missing.
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Mm-hmm.
Interviewer (Int.): So you just remember it, you don't do anything?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Yeah. I remember it, and then I practice it the right way until it becomes muscle memory.
Interviewer (Int.): So how quickly can you get ready for the next run-through with the same correction?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Instantly.
Interviewer (Int.): What if there are multiple corrections?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): I just try to keep them in my head. Fix what I can.
Interviewer (Int.): Can you remember a rehearsal where reading the music became difficult after playing for a long time?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): I mean, yeah. Usually after, like, if you're sitting there reading it for hours, then it just kind of sucks after a while.
Interviewer (Int.): So what made it difficult, you think?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): I don't know, like fatigue, almost.
Interviewer (Int.): Fatigue. Was the music too small, crowded, far away, or difficult to follow?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): No, just general fatigue.
Interviewer (Int.): Did you change anything to make it easier?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Maybe a sip of coffee or something.
Interviewer (Int.): Have you ever changed the appearance of your music to make it easier for you to read? Like enlarging, highlighting some parts, increasing the contrast, adding more colors, rewriting some sections, or adding note names or symbols throughout the notes?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): When I was learning melodic percussion, I would write notes on the page, or when I was learning bass drum, I would...
Interviewer (Int.): So newcomers might need that?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Yeah, definitely newcomers. Newcomers and wind players.
Interviewer (Int.): Have you ever bought something specifically to make reading or managing music easier? Like apps, subscriptions for MuseScore, tablet accessories, stands, devices, printing, or enlargement?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): No.
Interviewer (Int.): Any of your friends?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Not that I can think of. Usually for our pieces, we just rig something up. Like, if I have eight sheets of music, sometimes I'll just tape them to a piece of cardboard so I can just flip the whole thing halfway through the piece.
Interviewer (Int.): That's a little bit of work. What problem were you trying to solve?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): If you have a lot of sheets of music, you can generally just memorize the section around where the page turn is, and then you can turn the page whenever you get a chance. This is generally the strategy.
Interviewer (Int.): Okay. What is the most frustrating part of dealing with sheet music during rehearsal?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Paper sheet music, turning the page.
Interviewer (Int.): All right. What have you already tried to make that easier?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): That cardboard thing, putting it all together.
Interviewer (Int.): Which of these solutions actually helped? Which one did not? Is there any solution that you tried and did not work? Maybe it's the same one.
Maddux Vitrano (MV): The cardboard thing semi-works, depending on the piece and how long it is.
Interviewer (Int.): It cannot go infinite.
Maddux Vitrano (MV): Yeah, and also depending on how many stands you can have. Like, if I could have two stands, I could fit four pieces on the cardboard and flip the whole thing if there's a rest there. Otherwise, I have to memorize a bunch of stuff, and then when I'm not playing, then I can...
Interviewer (Int.): All right. So now let's wrap up. Is there anything about reading, preparing, or managing music that regularly bothers you, but that we did not discuss?
Maddux Vitrano (MV): No, not really. No, not that I can think of.
Interviewer (Int.): All right. It was a nice interview. Thank you.

INTERVIEW 5 — DR. LANDRY
========================================================================================
Interviewer (Int.): To start off, could you give me an overview of your ensembles and how many students you cover?
Dr. Landry (DL): There’s three main [ensembles].The largest one would be Marching Band, anywhere between what is now 160 students all the way up to as many as 300 a few years prior. Second would be Concert Band, an indoor concert ensemble anywhere between 80 to 110 students. The last one would be the smallest, which is Percussion Ensemble, that can be as few as 3 or 4 up to as many as 15 to 20.
Interviewer (Int.): So I assume the Percussion Ensemble covers some unique stuff?
Dr. Landry (DL): Oh, for sure we do.
Interviewer (Int.): What does your normal weekly rehearsal schedule look like, especially leading up to performances or competitions?
Dr. Landry (DL): Essentially, for all three of those ensembles, it’s two or three rehearsals a week. Especially in the case of Marching Band and Concert Band, it's three rehearsals a week. For Marching Band it’s two hours a day, Concert band is about 50 minutes a day. Percussion Ensemble is about two rehearsals a week, but because of the nature of the number of pieces we do, it may not be every single day. For example, if we have six pieces we’re working on, some students may rehearse one day a week, some may rehearse two, and some may do none at all one week and two the next week, so the schedule changes depending on the piece we’re working on.
Interviewer (Int.): Awesome. So is two pieces an average that y’all usually cover?
Dr. Landry (DL): Oh it can be more. We’ve done as many as 5 or 6 large ensembles plus three or four smaller ones, some steel band pieces, it can blow out to sometimes 20 pieces of music.
Interviewer (Int.): That’s quite a few.
Dr. Landry (DL): Yeah, but there’s a difference in the amount of time each piece takes. Steel band tunes take like three or four minutes, so you can do five of those in the span of 15, maybe 20 minutes max, whereas there’s some standalone pieces like one we’re doing right now that’s 11 minutes. We don’t always pin it down, so every semester looks very different.
Interviewer (Int.): I see. So how do you usually prepare and distribute the sheet music? For example, the last concert cycle you had, from selecting the pieces to the first rehearsal?
Dr. Landry (DL): For me, when I talk about Concert Band, I first think about “Are there pieces that I would like to personally play? Are there pieces that I think are important for our students to know as musicians–you know, pillars of wind band literature?” And then fun things, new music or composers that I want to introduce, you know, those types of things. So I’ll usually start formulating my program around this time of year [fall semester] for the following spring since we only meet then. Distribution usually occurs very close to the start of the semester, so it can be done either with an email saying “folders are done, pick them up,” maybe a week before classes begin, or at the first, second, or third rehearsal–somewhere in there–we will distribute music to the ensemble. Marching Band is different as we do most of our stuff digitally. We hand out no paper copies whatsoever. Percussion Ensemble is very similar to Concert Band as we usually begin the semester formulating our pieces and distribute them as we go on. For example this semester, we have some pieces planned that our students have not seen yet and we’re a month in. It’ll take some time for things to get out, but it’s also relative to when our final performance is. We want to make sure everyone has enough time to be prepared, to feel confident, and to execute the thing we want them to execute. That time can fluctuate.
Interviewer (Int.): Ok, so Concert Band and Percussion usually receive it in folders, while Marching Band receive it digitally. How is it usually distributed digitally?
Dr. Landry (DL): I can speak on drumline since that’s my primary focus. We have a protected site in Google Drive that we use to distribute parts. Those parts are my arrangements or the arrangements of my colleague for everything we do, from stands to halftime shows to warmup cadences. Students receive those sometimes as early as June for the coming Fall. They don’t have to print the PDFs, they can access them on their phones and tablets.
Interviewer (Int.): Say, for Concert Band, if you have a piece with a complex time signature, fast tempo changes, or some tricky intervals for students, how much time do you spend marking those up for students?
Dr. Landry (DL): I don’t mark anyone’s music. I mark on my score, but my goal is to teach students to recognize their problems for themselves to solve. The way I see it is I see them three times a week for 50 minutes. For the rest of the week, they see themselves in the practice rooms, so they need to diagnose those problems themselves before they show up to rehearsal. I’ll point them out, especially if there’s a particularly tricky rhythm or fingering, I’ll say “check out measure so-and-so, that rhythm’s complicated. Let’s take a moment to go over it really quick,” and that gives them a little more information, but it’s their responsibility to dig into the rest of it to completion. I’m only presenting the information for them to see and recognize it.
Interviewer (Int.): Do you have any students who need visual accommodations like large print or something high contrast to look at?
Dr. Landry (DL): We have a few. We’ve never dealt with high contrast, but I have had students that required large print. Int. So, about the last rehearsal you went over, if the ensemble stumbled at all or got out of sync in a terrible way, were there any significant causes easy to point out?
Dr. Landry (DL): To me there are two main ones. The first one is showing up to rehearsal without having looked at the music. They’re not prepared to listen to other people, they’re focusing on themselves and that’s where ensembles can fracture. The second thing, and this is partially for me to see and understand, is music that is beyond their current capabilities. My job as the instructor is to push and challenge them, but not to overwhelm them, and it’s a very thin line. It’s about finding that challenge point. The best way to do that is experience, but also having good mentors who can provide you with what worked for them. You can build a portfolio of challenge points that help you build a better program to challenge your students.
Interviewer (Int.): For those students who might not have looked at the music before, they were struggling to read it in real time.

Interviewer (Int.): You’d struggle unless you were a perfect public speaker?

Interviewer (Int.): And they have to go together.
Dr. Landry (DL): So all of these things have to stack up. If you’re just now looking at the music, you haven’t had a chance to consider how all the other parts interact. You’ll hear something weird and stop, not really sure how things fit together.
Interviewer (Int.): You spoke on this earlier, but when students miss something like an interval or key change it’s usually a “call out to write it down”?
Dr. Landry (DL): Sometimes I don’t say anything at all. Sometimes I trust a student will recognize it. In an ensemble you can almost see students go “Oh, I understand,” and it’s better in that moment not to labor in that point but simply give students the opportunity to try again.
Interviewer (Int.): Do you have any tools or specific call outs for those repeated errors?
Dr. Landry (DL): Nothing specific, we handle each situation as it happens. It would be like if I’m a carpenter and all I used was a hammer. Well, I might need to cut wood, just that tool won’t do much.

Dr. Landry (DL): That’s a good point to make. First thing to consider is slowing down. In practice, generally slowing down is better, especially at the beginning. I prefer they learn from their peers or seek out helpful references online, like watching someone play scales for techniques. If they still don’t get it, at that point I’ll step in and say “hey let’s talk about things we can do.” But I want them to discover what it is they’re actually doing instead of waiting for the answer. It’s never a good idea to be constantly giving answers in an ensemble. We talk about the process of doing things right, but not enough about learning through failure and mistakes. Dr. Landry (DL): Yes. Well, we abide by copyright laws and they have very specific rules. On a case-by-case basis we would deal with that.
Interviewer (Int.): What’s your policy for phones and tablets on stands during rehearsals?
Dr. Landry (DL): No problem. I assume my students are paying attention unless I’m given reason to believe otherwise. It’s easier to keep them engaged with how I teach and assume they’re paying attention rather than policing their phones. It’s often their section-mates that will nudge them and say “hey, pay attention.”
Interviewer (Int.): In the last year, have you had to purchase or approve any software, digital sheet music tools subscriptions for your program? If so, what was the approval process?
Dr. Landry (DL): I have not personally, no. If I purchase a piece of music, I buy it as myself for the university using our university funding. I buy nothing for the students except the pieces themselves or we write it in-house.

Interviewer (Int.): Is there anything about score readability or rehearsal flow that I didn’t ask but really grinds your gears?
Dr. Landry (DL): Not from an individual basis. I think most students can read their parts pretty well, but when you look at a full score for a large band, it can be a bit unwieldy since it’s a lot of information and generally not a big space.
Interviewer (Int.): (Signs a roughly 20:15 region midair for clarification)
Dr. Landry (DL): You would think it would be that size, but some can be smaller like the size of your laptop. What my friends and colleagues have done is resize their scores and bring them to a print shop to help with visibility. Also the more you do a piece, the less you’ll rely on a score.
